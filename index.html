Form Factor

Motherboard Formats

The motherboard determines which components fit in your build. Bigger boards offer more expansion slots; smaller ones save space.

Last updated: 06/2026
Full Size

ATX

The standard for most desktop builds. Plenty of PCIe slots, RAM slots, and room to grow.

Size: 305 × 244 mm

Mid Size

Micro-ATX

A balanced choice. Slightly smaller than ATX, still supports most CPUs and expansion cards at a lower price.

Size: 244 × 244 mm

Compact

Mini-ITX

Tiny footprint for small form factor cases. Limited to one PCIe slot and two RAM sticks — but impressively capable.

Size: 170 × 170 mm

Enthusiast

E-ATX

Extended ATX for workstations and HEDT platforms. Maximum expansion, dual CPU support on some boards.

Size: 305 × 330 mm

Memory

RAM Generations

System memory speed and capacity affect multitasking, creative workloads, and gaming frame rates.

Last updated: 06/2026
Current Gen

DDR5

The modern standard for modern platforms. Higher bandwidth and on-die ECC. Kits regularly baseline at 6000+ MT/s for optimal processing sweet-spots.

Voltage: 1.1 V · Max speed: 8400+ MT/s

Previous Gen

DDR4

Still widely used. Affordable, broadly compatible with legacy and budget configurations. Sweet spot: 16–32 GB at 3200–3600 MHz.

Voltage: 1.2 V · Max speed: ~5333 MT/s

Legacy

DDR3

Found in legacy setups. Fine for basic office use, but a bottleneck for modern heavy processing requirements.

Voltage: 1.5 V · Max speed: ~2133 MT/s

Very Old

DDR2

Severely limited bandwidth and old standard architecture — suitable only for classic legacy hardware preservation.

Voltage: 1.8 V · Max speed: ~1066 MT/s

Before You Build

Compatibility Checklist

Every build is a chain of matching parts. Get one link wrong and nothing fits — check these five pairings before you head to the builder.

Last updated: 05/2026
1

CPU ↔ Socket

Your CPU only fits one socket type (e.g. AM5, LGA1851). This is fixed by the CPU generation and can't be changed.

2

Socket ↔ Motherboard

The motherboard must use the exact same socket as your CPU — a mismatch means it physically won't fit.

3

Motherboard ↔ RAM Type

DDR4 and DDR5 slots aren't interchangeable. The motherboard's chipset decides which one your build supports.

4

Motherboard ↔ Case

ATX, Micro-ATX, Mini-ITX and E-ATX boards each need a case with matching mounting points and clearance.

5

CPU + GPU ↔ PSU Wattage

Add up your components' power draw and leave headroom. Use the calculator above to get a recommended wattage.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers to the questions we hear most often when people start planning a build.

Last updated: 07/2026
DDR4 or DDR5 — which RAM should I buy?

If your CPU and motherboard support DDR5, go with DDR5 — it's the current standard with more headroom for the future. DDR4 is still a solid, cheaper choice if you're building on an older or budget platform.

ATX or Micro-ATX — which format do I need?

ATX gives you more PCIe and RAM slots plus room to expand later. Micro-ATX is smaller and usually cheaper, and is plenty for most builds that don't need multiple expansion cards.

NVMe or SATA SSD — does it matter?

For your OS drive, NVMe is the better choice — it's dramatically faster and prices have come down. SATA SSDs are still fine for extra storage if you're on a tight budget.

How many watts do I need for my PSU?

Add up your CPU and GPU's rated power draw, then leave about 20-30% headroom for the rest of the system and future upgrades. Most single-GPU gaming builds land between 550W and 850W.

How much VRAM do I actually need?

8GB is the practical minimum for 1080p gaming today. If you're targeting 1440p or 4K, look for 12GB or more so textures don't become a bottleneck.
